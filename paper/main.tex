\title{\vspace{-1.5em}\textbf{The Output Budget Confound: Why Token Ceilings\\
Silently Decide LLM Planning Benchmark Results}}
\author{SlotSaver Team\\\small SteelHacks 2026}
\date{}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Comparisons between large language models on planning benchmarks are usually
reported as a single accuracy number per model. We show that for models which
reason in the open, this number is not a property of the model alone: it is
jointly determined by the output token ceiling the evaluator happened to choose.
On the Calendar Scheduling task of \textsc{Natural Plan} \cite{zheng2024naturalplan},
raising the ceiling from \tokenLowBudget{} to \tokenHighBudget{} tokens moves
\nemotronName{} from \nemotronLowAcc{} to \nemotronHighAcc{} with the prompt, the
sample and the scoring held fixed. The model was not failing to plan. At the
lower ceiling \nemotronTruncLow{} of its generations were cut off before an
answer was emitted, and an exact match scorer recorded that truncation as a
planning error. We quantify the effect across tier matched models, show that its
size tracks how verbosely a model reasons, and separate it from test time
compute scaling by reporting the truncation rate alongside accuracy. We further
show that the obvious way to detect truncation, comparing emitted tokens against
the ceiling, is unsound for models that reason in hidden tokens and fails in the
direction of understating them. We
argue that the generation ceiling is a confound evaluation reports must state,
and that a truncation rate materially above zero invalidates the accuracy number
it accompanies. We release the harness and the per call records.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Planning under constraints is an attractive target for language model
evaluation: solutions are hard to find and cheap to verify. \textsc{Natural
Plan} \cite{zheng2024naturalplan} exploits exactly this, scoring a proposed
meeting time by exact match against a golden answer, with no human annotation
and no model acting as judge. That property is why we selected it. It removes
the dominant failure mode of bespoke benchmarks, in which the party reporting a
comparison also authored its ground truth.

Having removed that confound, we encountered another. Contemporary models differ
sharply in how much text they emit before committing to an answer. A model that
reasons silently answers in tens of tokens. A model that reasons in the open may
spend thousands. Every evaluation harness imposes a generation ceiling. When
that ceiling falls below what a model needs in order to finish, the harness
records no answer, and an exact match scorer, which cannot distinguish an absent
answer from an incorrect one, records a planning failure.

This is not hypothetical. In our first run, \nemotronName{} scored
\nemotronLowAcc{} at a ceiling of \tokenLowBudget{} tokens. Inspecting the raw
generations showed the model mid derivation at the cut, having already correctly
enumerated every participant constraint. At \tokenHighBudget{} tokens, with
nothing else changed, the same model on the same items scored
\nemotronHighAcc{}. Had we published the first number, we would have reported a
false result in good faith, and the harness, not the model, would have been at
fault.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item We isolate the generation ceiling as an experimental variable on
      \textsc{Natural Plan} Calendar Scheduling and measure its effect on two
      tier matched models chosen to sit at opposite ends of output verbosity.
\item We show the effect is not uniform across models, so a single ceiling
      applied to every model is not a neutral experimental choice, and we
      separate it from test time compute scaling by reporting the truncation
      rate and the solve rate conditional on finishing.
\item We show that the obvious way to detect truncation, comparing emitted
      tokens against the ceiling, is unsound for models that reason in hidden
      tokens, and that it fails in the direction of understating them
      (Section~\ref{sec:hidden}).
\item We report accuracy jointly with output spend and latency, validate our
      scorer against published numbers before reporting any comparison, and
      quantify run to run variance from a seeded replication.
\item We release the harness, the per call records and the analysis code.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Planning benchmarks.} \textsc{Natural Plan} \cite{zheng2024naturalplan}
evaluates trip, meeting and calendar planning expressed in natural language with
programmatically verifiable answers. PlanBench \cite{valmeekam2023planbench}
makes a comparable argument in a PDDL setting. Both report accuracy per model
without treating the generation ceiling as a variable.

\textbf{Test time compute.} That additional generation improves reasoning
accuracy is well established \cite{wei2022chain,snell2024scaling}. Our claim is
narrower and methodological. When the ceiling is set below a model's natural
output length, the resulting number is an artifact of harness configuration
rather than a measurement of capability, and any report that omits the ceiling
is not reproducible.

\textbf{Contamination.} Public benchmarks are absorbed into training corpora
\cite{sainz2023nlpcontamination}, motivating rolling benchmarks such as
LiveCodeBench \cite{jain2024livecodebench}. We treat contamination as a stated
limitation rather than a solved problem; see Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Task and scoring}
We use the Calendar Scheduling split of \textsc{Natural Plan}: \datasetSize{}
items, each giving the availability of every participant and a required meeting
duration, with exactly one valid slot. Difficulty is controlled by participant
count, which ranges from two to seven. Scoring follows the official evaluator. A
regular expression extracts the first \texttt{Day, HH:MM - HH:MM} span from the
generation and compares day, start and end against the golden answer.

\subsection{Scorer validation}
Before comparing any models we validated our port of the official evaluator
against the reference predictions shipped with the dataset. Our implementation
scores those predictions at \geminiPublishedRepro{}, against
\geminiPublishedPaper{} reported in the original work, so our numbers sit on the
same scale as the published ones. We note that the shipped prediction field is
named for a shot count whose published accuracy differs from what the field
actually scores. We therefore set shot count explicitly in all of our own runs
rather than relying on that label.

\subsection{Models}
Our primary comparison is between two models matched by vendor tier and chosen
because they sit at opposite ends of output verbosity, which is the axis the
effect acts on: \nemotronName{}, a sparse mixture of experts
\cite{shazeer2017outrageously} with \nemotronTotalParams{} total and
\nemotronActiveParams{} active parameters, which reasons in its visible output;
and \claudeName{}, which is comparatively terse. Only the first has a published
parameter count, so we do not plot accuracy against parameter count, because
doing so for the other would require inventing a number.

We additionally report a small set of \geminiName{} results. These are
preliminary and we label them as such: the free API tier for that model permits
twenty requests per day, which is not a sample size, and we had no funded
account. We include them only because they bear on the measurement issue in
Section~\ref{sec:hidden}, not as a third arm of the comparison.

\subsection{Detecting truncation is harder than it looks}
\label{sec:hidden}
The obvious way to detect that a generation hit the ceiling is to compare the
emitted token count against it. That test is wrong for any model that reasons
in hidden tokens. In our \geminiName{} runs, generations were visibly cut in
mid sentence, including mid answer, while the reported output token count stood
at roughly twenty against a ceiling of \tokenLowBudget{}. The reasoning tokens
are billed against the ceiling but are absent from the visible output count, so
the naive check reports no truncation precisely where truncation is worst.

We flag this because it is a trap with an unpleasant shape: the check fails
silently, it fails in the direction that makes a model look worse rather than
better, and it fails specifically on the reasoning models this effect concerns.
We therefore take the vendor's finish reason as authoritative wherever it is
available, and treat any token based rule as a fallback. Our earlier analysis
used the token rule and, on that basis alone, would have reported \geminiName{}
as unaffected by the ceiling. The raw generations show the opposite.

\subsection{Experimental controls}
Every model receives the byte identical user prompt taken from the dataset, with
no per model prompt engineering, so the comparison measures the model rather
than our prompt writing. One deliberate asymmetry exists and we state it
plainly: \nemotronName{} is sent the vendor documented reasoning control
(\texttt{detailed thinking off}) in a system turn, which the other two models do
not receive because they have no equivalent. We use it because running a model
outside its documented configuration is the less defensible choice, but it is
not a neutral difference and a reader should know it is there.

We note in passing that this control did not do what its name suggests. With
\texttt{detailed thinking off} set, \nemotronName{} still emitted extended
derivations, averaging thousands of output tokens per item, which is precisely
what makes it vulnerable to the ceiling effect. Practitioners relying on that
toggle to bound output length should verify it empirically rather than trust it.

Sampling temperature is zero wherever the vendor SDK exposes it.

Calls that never reached the model are excluded from every solve rate and
reported separately as an error count. Under load the NVIDIA endpoint returns
HTTP 429 and 503 at a substantial rate, and we retry with exponential backoff,
but a call that exhausts its retries produced no generation to score. Placing it
in the denominator would record an infrastructure failure as a planning failure,
which is the same conflation this paper is about. We note that our own first
analysis did exactly that before we caught it, which we take as evidence that
the mistake is easy rather than unusual. Latency is measured around the successful request only, excluding our own
retry backoff, so that rate limiting imposed by our billing tier is not charged
to a model's speed. Items are sampled stratified by participant count, because
the raw distribution is half two participant items and a uniform sample would
largely measure the easiest bucket. Uncertainty is reported as Wilson score
intervals \cite{wilson1927probable}, which behave correctly near the boundaries
where a normal approximation does not.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The generation ceiling effect}
Figure~\ref{fig:budget}(a) sweeps the generation ceiling with every other
variable held fixed. \nemotronName{} rises from \nemotronLowAcc{} to
\nemotronHighAcc{}. The comparison models, which emit far less text before
answering, are substantially flatter across the same sweep. A single ceiling
applied uniformly is therefore not neutral: it penalises verbose reasoners
specifically, and the size of the penalty is a property of the harness rather
than of the task.

\claudeName{} is not perfectly monotonic across the sweep: it dips slightly at
an intermediate ceiling. That dip is not statistically distinguishable from
noise on the paired items (exact McNemar, $p = 0.15$), its truncation rate is
already zero at that ceiling so no ceiling effect is available to explain it,
and it is the one model we could not pin to temperature zero because its SDK no
longer exposes the parameter. We flag it rather than smooth it, and we do not
read anything into it.

Figure~\ref{fig:budget}(b) shows the mechanism directly. We record the fraction
of generations that terminate at the ceiling rather than at a stop token. At the
\tokenLowBudget{} token ceiling, \nemotronTruncLow{} of \nemotronName{}
generations are cut off, against \claudeTruncLow{} for \claudeName{}. At
\tokenHighBudget{} tokens the figure falls to \nemotronTruncHigh{}. The
accuracy curve in panel (a) is the mirror image of the truncation curve in panel
(b), which is what distinguishes this from a test time compute effect: the
model is not reasoning better with more tokens, it is being permitted to finish
at all.

\subsection{Model comparison, and what counts as an adequate ceiling}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports all three models at the highest ceiling we ran.
Alongside the headline solve rate we report two columns that an evaluation would
not normally include: the truncation rate, and the solve rate computed over only
those generations that were allowed to finish.

The second column is the one that matters for interpreting the first. A
generation that is cut off and a generation that proposes the wrong meeting time
are the same event to an exact match scorer, but they are completely different
events for anyone deciding which model to deploy. Separating them shows that
\nemotronName{} remains truncated on \nemotronTruncHigh{} of items even at
\tokenHighBudget{} tokens, so its headline number is still an underestimate at a
ceiling most practitioners would consider generous. We therefore resist the
phrase ``adequate ceiling'' as an absolute: adequacy is a property of a model
and a harness together, and the only way to know a ceiling was adequate is to
measure the truncation rate and find it at zero.

\section{Discussion}

The practical recommendation is small and cheap. Evaluation reports should state
the generation ceiling alongside accuracy, and should verify that the ceiling
binds on no model before comparing any two. A one line check suffices, and we
report it in Figure~\ref{fig:budget}(b): the fraction of generations that
terminate at the ceiling rather than at a stop token. A model whose truncation
rate is materially above zero has not been measured, it has been cut off, and
its accuracy number carries no information about its planning ability. In our
own first run that fraction was \nemotronTruncLow{} for a model we were about to
report at \nemotronLowAcc{}.

The distinction from test time compute scaling \cite{snell2024scaling} matters
here. That literature asks how accuracy improves when a model is given more room
to think. We are pointing at something more basic and more embarrassing: below a
threshold, the harness is not measuring thinking at all, because no answer is
ever emitted. The two effects are separable precisely by the truncation rate,
which is why we argue it belongs in evaluation reports as a matter of routine.

The effect interacts badly with two current trends. Models increasingly reason
in the open, and harnesses increasingly cap output in order to control cost.
Those pressures push in opposite directions, and the resulting headline number
is quietly sensitive to a parameter that is rarely reported at all.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Contamination.} \textsc{Natural Plan} has been public since 2024 and may
appear in the training data of any model evaluated here. We do not claim
decontamination. The exposure applies to all three models, so we treat the cross
model comparison as contamination symmetric rather than contamination free, and
we regard the ceiling result, which is a within model effect, as the more robust
of our two findings.

\textbf{Temperature.} The Anthropic Python SDK version used here removed
\texttt{temperature} from its message creation call, so \claudeName{} runs at the
API default while the other two run at zero. This is an SDK constraint. We state
it rather than omit it.

\textbf{Endpoints, not weights.} All three models are accessed through hosted
APIs. Vendor side changes are not observable to us, so these results are tied to
the run date recorded in Section~\ref{sec:repro}.

\textbf{Sample loss correlates with the swept variable.} The provider returned
429 and 503 at a rate that rose with the ceiling, because a larger ceiling means
longer generations and therefore more concurrent requests in flight. For
\nemotronName{} the loss was zero at \tokenLowBudget{} tokens and rose steadily
with the ceiling, so the higher ceiling cells rest on fewer retained items and
carry correspondingly wider intervals. We report the retained count for every
cell rather than only the rate. We do not believe this biases the direction of
the effect, since a dropped call is a call that produced no output at all and is
independent of whether that output would have been correct, but it does cost
precision exactly where we would most like to have it.

\textbf{Scope.} One task from one benchmark. We do not claim the ceiling effect
generalises to tasks that do not elicit extended reasoning, although the
mechanism that produces it is not task specific.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}

All runs were executed on \runDate{}. The harness, the per call records
including raw generations, and the analysis code are released alongside this
paper. Sampling is seeded and stratified, and the seed is recorded in every
result file. Scoring uses our validated port of the official evaluator.

\paragraph{Run to run stability.} An interrupted earlier sweep had already
scored part of the sample before it was stopped. Because sampling is seeded, the
relaunched sweep drew the same items, giving an independent replication at no
additional cost. Across \replPairs{} model and ceiling pairs, the two runs agreed
on at least \replWorstAgree{} of items individually, and no pair was separable
by a McNemar test. We report this because an evaluation that prints one accuracy
figure per model gives a reader no way to judge how much of a reported gap is
run to run noise, and in our case the ceiling effect is an order of magnitude
larger than that noise while the between model differences are not.

\section{Conclusion}

A generation ceiling is not a neutral implementation detail of an evaluation
harness. For models that reason in the open it can move a headline accuracy
number by a wide margin, and it does so silently, because a truncated generation
and a wrong answer are indistinguishable to an exact match scorer. We recommend
that benchmark reports state the ceiling, verify that it binds on no model, and
publish output length alongside accuracy.
